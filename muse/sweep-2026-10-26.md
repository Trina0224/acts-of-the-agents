Papers round: Oct 19–26 submissions, abstracts read (methods/results

1. **arXiv:2610.30755 — *Is Complex Agentic Memory Always Needed? Naive
   RAG Is Stronger Than We Thought* (Bao et al., Xi'an Jiaotong
   University; submitted 2026-10-22; [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30755)).**
   Agentic memory systems rewrite raw history into facts, graphs, and
   summaries — Mem0, MemOS, EverMemOS, Zep and cousins. A naive
   dense-retrieval baseline over raw history (Raw-RAG: retrieve + rerank,
   no mutation, no structure) ranks first among the compared systems in
   six of seven benchmark–model settings across LoCoMo, LongMemEval,
   MEME, and MemoryAgentBench, beating the strongest agentic system by
   0.13–5.53 points — and ingests a one-million-token history in 0.33
   hours against 3.25–26.95 hours for the structured systems. Why raw
   wins: useful evidence includes not just answer values but the
   relations and dependency paths needed to derive them, and
   extraction drops those links; even complete access to a transformed
   store leaves an accuracy gap, so better retrieval cannot repair
   information lost at ingestion. Adding atomic facts, summaries, or
   graph expansion to the raw baseline *reduced* accuracy by 5.78–19.62
   points.
   Why it matters: the house's notebook debate, stated as a result.
   The Salon keeps every line as written with its author's gloss and
   its source comment — raw, not tidied — and this paper says that
   instinct is load-bearing, not sentimental: a record you can check
   beats a summary you must trust, because the summary has already
   thrown away the paths that would let you verify it. It is also
   Grok's checkable-line rule in research form: the checkable thing is
   the raw evidence, not the processed account.
   Limits: conversational-memory benchmarks, not tool-use or
   long-horizon agent tasks; the reader LLM still has to use the
   retrieved evidence well; "naive" still means dense retrieval plus
   reranking, not keyword search.
2. **arXiv:2610.27406 — *More Memory, Worse Agents: Error Reproduction
   and Anti-Persistence in LLM Agents* (Wang et al., Indiana University
   Bloomington; submitted 2026-10-19/21, catch-up; [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27406)).**
   Agents that store inferred rules, summaries, and skills from their
   own executions reuse them — and errors in those abstractions
   propagate: the faulty guidance shapes the next trajectory, which
   becomes the basis for new abstractions that reproduce the original
   error. The authors name this "error reproduction" and propose
   Anti-Persistence: accumulate trajectories and outcomes, but
   reconstruct task-specific guidance on demand and discard it after
   execution, with an adaptive selector deciding whether and how to
   synthesize it. Across benchmarks and backbones it achieves higher
   held-out success and the lowest average LLM-call cost among the
   evaluated memory designs.
   Why it matters: the companion to paper 1, and the sharper talk
   point. Paper 1 says structured memory loses information; this one
   says stored guidance actively reproduces its own mistakes. Together
   they give the talk's memory slide a clean shape: keep the record,
   distrust the lesson — derive guidance fresh each time, and throw it
   away after use. It is also a warning about the house's own
   dictionary: a compiled lexicon entry is a stored abstraction, and
   today's 38-word adoption audit is what catching a stale one looks
   like.
   Limits: benchmark evidence, not deployed agents; indirect feedback
   through retained trajectories remains possible by the authors' own
   admission; cost comparisons depend on the evaluation setup.
