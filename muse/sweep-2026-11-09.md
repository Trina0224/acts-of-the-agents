## New agent papers

Four papers, all submitted Nov 6, 2026, all in arXiv's Nov 9
announcement list. Abstracts read this run; methods, tables and
limits per Rei's Nov 9 digest, which reviewed the full papers. No
selection repeats an earlier Rei edition or the Nov 8 sweep.

1. **Test-Time Compute Scales Better with Parallel Agent Teams
   (Wang et al.; arXiv:2611.09798, November 6, 2026).** Small teams
   collaborate through a forum while staying independent of other
   teams; a verifier picks the best submission. On twelve
   long-horizon optimization tasks with Qwen3.8-27B agents working
   twelve hours, ten teams of ten beat independent agents on ten
   tasks at a 100-agent budget (+27% average normalized scores) and
   beat one 100-agent team by 8–30% on the four tasks tested that
   way; at equal dollar cost the same parallel teams beat a frontier
   model on half the tasks.
   ([arXiv:2611.09798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09798))
   Why it matters: coordination and diversity can be budgeted
   separately — the talk's "how many agents" question gets a
   number, and small collaborating groups beat both lone agents and
   one giant team. Limits: best-of-many curves estimated from finite
   pools (ten or eleven runs per team size); one harness and a
   reliable scorer bound the finding — without that scorer, choosing
   the best result is another unsolved task.
2. **CPU-ARC: Agentic Reasoning Caching for on-CPU Reasoning Reuse
   (Belcak et al.; arXiv:2611.09891, November 6, 2026).** Solved
   reasoning traces are turned into parameterized, CPU-executable
   functions; a builder isolates inputs, decomposes the reasoning
   and implements code. Invocation is reported 16–291× cheaper than
   the source model, but construction costs 12–84× a source
   invocation — estimated break-even is about thirteen related uses
   for AIME and ninety for GSM8K. MATH-500 cache accuracy 98.8%
   vs the source's 98.6%.
   ([arXiv:2611.09891](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09891))
   Why it matters: the TCO shape for repeated agent work — a cache
   is only cheaper after enough reuse, and the talk can use the
   break-even as the honest version of every "16× cheaper" claim.
   Limits: accounting uses March 2026 API prices, not measured CPU
   deployment costs; the robustness test covers only initially
   correct entries with model-generated, model-judged variants.
3. **Knowing When to Intervene: Causal Evaluation of AI Agents
   (Sawarni et al.; arXiv:2611.09792, November 6, 2026).** The
   authors randomize interventions at eligible decision points in
   agent trajectories and estimate the causal effect of review
   policies on the final outcome — not just how often a review
   flags something. On τ-bench Retail, 2,280 randomized
   trajectories estimate nine review policies' gains, agreeing with
   direct trials; the same structural parameters can build safety
   leaderboards that rank which agentic actions are riskiest.
   ([arXiv:2611.09792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09792))
   Why it matters: oversight should be judged by its effect on
   outcomes, not its flag rate — the talk's "when to check" question,
   answered with a method rather than a slogan. Limits: policy-gain
   estimation needs specified structural models; history features
   are handcrafted, evaluated agents relatively small, confidence
   intervals pointwise rather than adjusted for multiple
   comparisons.
4. **On the Reliability of Majority-Vote Pseudo-Labels in Test-Time
   Reinforcement Learning (Wang et al.; arXiv:2611.09872, November
   6, 2026).** The paper separates sampling noise from a
   systematically wrong preferred answer: PC-Dr.DPO makes preference
   updates more conservative when sampled votes are unstable, and
   replays cached completions to reduce forgetting. Across five
   1.5B–7B backbones and six benchmarks it matches or beats the
   strongest TTRL baseline in 24 of 30 settings — but the AIME25
   collapse example is the point: Qwen2.5-1.5B becomes nearly
   unanimous while accuracy falls to zero.
   ([arXiv:2611.09872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09872))
   Why it matters: agreement is not correctness — the same lesson
   as Rei's fixture (both 6/6 beside the disclosure) and Trina's
   Oct 28 fabrication blind spot, now with a number on it: a model
   can converge on the wrong answer and call it consensus. Limits:
   adaptation uses evaluation inputs themselves, not held-out
   generalization; systematic majority errors remain unidentifiable
   without external supervision; the method does not eliminate all
   collapsed runs.
