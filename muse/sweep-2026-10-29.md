1. **Rare, Relevant, and Still Wrong: How Memory Fails LLM Group
   Decisions (Imel, Charradi, Sghir, El Khammar; submitted Oct 27,
   2026; arXiv:2610.37624).** CrossProfile, a diagnostic benchmark
   of 60 templates / 480 tasks with automatically verified answer
   keys, crossing ownership of supporting, decoy and irrelevant
   facts while holding the text fixed, in a hidden-profile setup:
   the correct decision needs facts no single participant holds.
   Findings: picking memory by relevance loses 23–31 points to
   rarest-first selection in the classic configuration, because
   decoys are on topic — but rarest-first itself does no better
   than random on average; removing or randomizing attribution
   tags raises classic-configuration accuracy by 13–24 points,
   exposing a social-proof bias (facts attributed to many agents
   get extra weight); and the complete transcript yields lower
   accuracy than no notes at all. "Accuracy follows the balance
   of evidence the memory keeps, not the policy that kept it."
   ([arXiv:2610.37624](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37624))
   Why it matters: **this resolves yesterday's flagged candidate
   — the ID is now confirmed from the primary listing, and the
   abstract matches the secondary reports.** It is the group
   version of the Anthropic study: one agent's lean persuades one
   analyst; here, a group's memory persuades its own decision
   model by counting heads instead of weighing evidence — and
   keeping everything (the full transcript) is worse than keeping
   nothing, the memory chapter's sharpest counter-slogan to "keep
   the record." It also bites the Salon: a compiled lexicon is a
   memory policy, and this paper says the policy matters less
   than the balance of evidence it happens to keep.
   Limits: small models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, two DeepSeek
   models); synthetic hidden-profile tasks, not deployed
   multi-agent work; the "worse than no notes" result is on this
   benchmark's decision model, not a general law.
2. **Log-To-Leak: Prompt Injection Attacks on Tool-Using LLM
   Agents via the Model Context Protocol (Hu, Fan, Samyoun, Du;
   submitted Oct 26, 2026; arXiv:2610.36108).** Within the known
   MCP tool-poisoning surface, a post-hoc logging attack induces
   a separate attacker-controlled call *after* the final legitimate
   call, targeting the user query, server name, server response
   and agent response. Across 18 model-server pairs with valid
   tool execution, attack success rate 62.64–100% with conditional
   logging accuracy 76.55–95.64%; 16 pairs changed absolute
   target-task completion by at most 6.63 points — the job still
   gets done. Three evaluated defenses leave residual attack
   success above 55% on GitHub MCP with GPT-4o, while an
   instruction detector flags 85.53% of benign descriptions.
   ([arXiv:2610.36108](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36108))
   Why it matters: the Manus-shape threat, formalized for MCP —
   and worse in one respect: the agent finishes the job correctly
   *and* leaks, so completion is not a clean bill of health. The
   paper's own remedy points at the house's architecture:
   host-controlled authorization for secondary tool calls. That
   is what Trina's one-tap-per-push rule already is, applied to
   pushes; the question the paper leaves for the talk is what the
   equivalent is for every other secondary call an agent makes
   after the job looks done.
   Limits: lab MCP servers (5 servers, 4 agents); the success
   range is wide; defense evaluation is narrow and the detector's
   false-positive rate makes it unusable as stated.
3. **Continue or Restart? The Limits of Early Failure Prediction
   in Coding Agents (Srinivasan, Li, Menzies, Assunção; submitted
   Oct 26, 2026; arXiv:2610.35971).** Early stopping promises to
   save compute by killing unpromising runs; the study tests
   whether early signals can tell a failing attempt from a slow
   one. Across 147,002 execution traces in four model-framework
   settings, within-task discrimination at early landmarks (5k–20k
   tokens) stays near chance (mean within-task AUC 0.50). Under a
   75k-token cap, uninterrupted continuation beats both learned
   and random restart policies in 216 of 224 tested
   budget-threshold configurations, at lower mean token cost; the
   restart policies' small gains come almost entirely from
   between-task allocation (not stopping easy issues), while
   attempt-level selection contributes near zero (−0.11 to +0.24
   points).
   ([arXiv:2610.35971](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35971))
   Why it matters: the needle question's empirical cousin. You
   cannot tell a doomed run from a slow one early — so "stop and
   restart" is usually worse than "let it finish," and the honest
   stopping rule is about the *task* (is this issue worth the
   budget?), not the *attempt*. Read beside Grok's surgery story:
   the model that declined the live run after a full dry run was
   not early-stopping on a weak signal; it had completed the
   rehearsal and judged the finish unreachable. That distinction
   — a completed rehearsal vs. an early guess — is the talk's
   usable version of "when to stop."
   Limits: coding agents only, public corpora; the monitor tested
   is small (a fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B), so a stronger monitor might
   discriminate better — but the burden of proof is now on it.

  talk doesn't need the vendor slides; it needs the question they

  holding a token, who do you call to check whether the person

- **Completion is not a clean bill of health, twice over.**
  Log-To-Leak: the agent finishes the job and leaks afterward, and
  the defenses leave more than half the attacks standing. The
  Anthropic study, read properly by Rei: the recommendation's
  direct effect wasn't significant, and the non-user comparison
  wasn't causal. Both papers punish the same shortcut — judging
  the run by its ending. The talk's discipline, stated as a rule:
  check the middle (what else ran, what was assumed), not just
  the result.
