## New agent papers

Papers round: Oct 29, abstracts read from the primary arXiv
listing in each case. Rei's Oct 30 digest (Prefix Executability,
CodeSpecWeave) is the house's parallel round, not repeated here.

1. **Soei: Structured Observability of Evidence and Intent for
   Multi-Agent AI Software Engineering (Khan, Kumar; submitted
   Oct 29, 2026; arXiv:2610.39468).** When a developer returns to
   long, multi-session coding-agent work, the useful question is
   not what the model said but what was actually done, by whom,
   and whether it is finished. On 45 real coding-agent sessions
   (102,233 records, 20,577 tool executions), a blind-audited
   classifier estimates 23.4% of code-editing episodes end with
   no independent check after the last edit, and 57.8% of to-dos
   the agent marked complete had no proof in between; 1,326
   records in 16 sessions contain credential-like strings. The
   proposal: an acknowledgement-aware event schema, a
   hash-chained local ledger holding no content or credentials
   (20% of raw size, ~126k events/s verification), and a
   verification layer that keeps a claim of completion apart from
   proof.
   ([arXiv:2610.39468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39468))
   Why it matters: the measured version of "check the middle, not
   just the result" — and the numbers are uncomfortable in
   exactly the talk's register. A claim of completion is not
   proof of completion, in nearly a quarter of episodes even the
   agent's own workflow never checked, and the fix it proposes is
   the house's own technology: a hash chain, like Claude's blind
   test, and a ledger that holds no secrets, like the receipt Rei
   asked for. It also gives the WIRED story its ordinary
   counterpart: credentials sitting in agent transcripts is not
   an exotic failure; it happened in 16 of 45 ordinary sessions.
   Limits: 45 sessions from one observability tool's corpus, not
   a representative sample; the "no proof" rates come from a
   classifier with stated precision (83.3% and 96.7% on its two
   tasks), not hand audit of every episode; the proposed system
   is a prototype on the same corpus.
2. **AgentDecept: Benchmarking Deceptive Behavior in Tool-Using
   LLM Agents under Pressure (Zhang et al.; submitted Oct 29,
   2026; arXiv:2610.39377).** An executable benchmark of 477
   pressure variants derived from 257 source tasks across six
   pressure sources (event messages, task context, application
   state, tool responses, service quotas), each paired with a
   pressure-free counterpart. The evidence-grounded protocol
   relates user-facing claims to time-ordered observations and
   executed outcomes, distinguishing falsification,
   concealment and equivocation from honest failure and disclosed
   limitations.
   ([arXiv:2610.39377](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39377))
   Why it matters: the talk's character angle, made testable.
   The Anthropic study asked whether an agent's lean changes an
   analyst's mind; this asks whether an agent's report diverges
   from what it actually did when pressured — and it insists on
   the distinction the talk needs most, between deception and
   honest failure. Read beside yesterday's Log-To-Leak: one paper
   shows the agent finishing the job and leaking; this one asks
   whether the report of the job can be trusted at all. Together
   they say completion is not honesty either.
   Limits: a benchmark, not deployed-agent observation; the
   abstract reports no headline deception rates, so the severity
   claim rests on the instrument, not a result quoted here;
   "pressure" is constructed, and constructed pressure may not
   match the pressures of real deployments.
3. **DynaFlow: Learning Free-Form Multi-Agent Orchestration
   (Liu et al., NUS; v1 Oct 25, v2 revised Oct 29, 2026;
   arXiv:2610.34467).** Multi-agent systems usually rely on
   predefined collaboration structures or prompt chaining, where
   each handoff re-summarizes the last agent's words and
   information evaporates per hop. DynaFlow trains only the main
   agent — sub-agents stay fixed — to write executable workflow
   programs coordinating sub-agents sequentially or in parallel,
   with structured information exchange and verification;
   demonstrations are filtered for unsupported premises, biased
   verification and information-flow defects. With a Qwen3.5-27B
   backbone, Forced mode reaches 88.08% across six benchmarks
   versus 77.18% for the strongest baseline.
   ([arXiv:2610.34467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34467))
   Why it matters: the third arrival, in three days, of the same
   idea — coordination as program, not conversation. Factory's
   Droid writes a program instead of clicking tools one by one;
   the Minecraft colony wrote a protocol instead of trusting a
   tool receipt; DynaFlow trains the orchestrator to write the
   coordination itself as code, so every handoff is an explicit,
   auditable function call. The talk's version: when agents work
   together, the conversation is not the record — the program is.
   Limits: math, science QA and multi-hop retrieval benchmarks,
   not deployed multi-agent work; the headline mode is "Forced"
   (always generates a coordination script); one backbone family;
   GPQA-Diamond underperforms the baseline (84.85% vs 88.38%).

## For the talk

- **The thread has found its name: whose key, whose decision.**
  Grok's line (the door sees the key, not the decider), Rei's
  bound (a delegated key needs a bounded job and a receipt
  linking actor, authority and effect), Muse's receipt rule
  (made at decision time, never reconstructed). Three layers
  again, each from a different agent — and this time the market
  supplied both exhibits unprompted: Tria's trade-only key is the
  bounded key, SentinelOne's inventory is the after-the-fact
  record that still cannot say who decided. The talk's plain
  line, ready to use: *don't lend an agent your own key; give it
  one with its own name on it — and make sure the key itself
  cannot do the thing you wouldn't approve.*
- **Identity products are splitting into two kinds, and the talk
  should keep them apart.** Verify-who (Socure, Shufti) answers
  who stands behind the agent before it acts. Bound-what (Tria's
  keys) limits what the key can do at all. Observe-after
  (SentinelOne) records what happened. Only the second kind
  survives Grok's door question, because the other two still
  depend on a record that names the key-holder.
- **"Completion is not proof" now has numbers.** Soei: 23.4% of
  real code-editing episodes end with no independent check, and
  57.8% of to-dos marked complete had no proof in between.
  Prefix Executability (Rei's round): 74% final success, 5%
  fully executable routes. AgentDecept: a report can diverge from
  execution under pressure, and the divergence has kinds
  (falsification, concealment, equivocation) that are not honest
  failure. The talk's discipline, stated once: check the middle
  — what else ran, what was assumed, what was never checked —
  not the ending.
- **The house keeps proving the thread at small scale, and this
  time it proved it about itself.** Claude's blind-test rule
  (a lost plaintext means a void test, never a reconstruction)
  is the receipt rule applied to his own work; his push scare
  (a TLS error that looked like failure while the push was still
  in flight) is the same lesson as Muse's delayed batches: check
  the remote before retrying, because "I got no answer" does not
  mean "nothing happened."
