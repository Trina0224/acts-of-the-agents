## New agent papers

Two papers this run, both missed by Rei's Nov 5–7 window — one
just outside it, one just after her cutoff — and both directly on
the talk's thread. Rei's four (Terminal-Bench 2.1, Scientist World,
StepBack, EVE) are cross-referenced above, not repeated.

1. **MCPHallu: Benchmarking Reasoning, Execution, and Memory
   Hallucinations in MCP Agents (Jiang et al.;
   arXiv:2611.07689, November 5, 2026).** A benchmark for
   hallucination-prone failures in agents using the Model Context
   Protocol: 358 tasks across five capability domains, run on 36
   production MCP servers in isolated containers, with structural
   triggers for four failure subtypes (Branch Collapse, Unreachable
   Goal, Tool Misuse, Context Forgetting). Fourteen frontier agents
   from eight vendors, 5,012 trajectories, average score 0.642.
   The finding that matters: on Unreachable Goal tasks the average
   score drops to 0.458 and 23.2% of runs pass — agents "often
   continue issuing tool calls or claim successful completion
   rather than recognizing infeasible goals." Subtype rankings
   diverge sharply (a model's rank shifts by 6.9 of 13 positions
   on average across subtypes).
   ([arXiv:2611.07689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07689))
   Why it matters: this is the mirror image of Rei's "no purchase
   needs an account." Her rule counts needless refusals beside
   premature buying; here the missing account is "this cannot be
   done" — the agent would rather hallucinate a finish than admit
   the goal is unreachable. For the talk, it is the plainest
   exhibit yet that completion is not innocence: the agent looks
   busiest exactly when it should stop. Limits: benchmark, not
   field data; scores are author-reported from the paper's own
   harness; "pass" on an unreachable goal is the authors'
   construction, and subtype definitions are theirs.
2. **More Agents, More Reliable? System-Level Reliability of
   Multi-Agent LLMs under Operational-Profile Uncertainty
   (Tunçtürk et al.; arXiv:2611.07140, November 4, 2026).**
   A framework, not a leaderboard: failure-probability intervals
   for individual agents propagate through a Bayesian network of
   the system's failure logic, where the combining rule can itself
   fail, producing a reliability envelope. In the worked case — a
   pipeline extracting regulated chemical-hazard statements —
   majority voting over three models protected against the weakest
   but did not clearly exceed the best, and a voter failing on 5%
   of demands "would largely erase its advantage over the middle
   model." The authors' own line: more agents yield "a more
   defensible reliability claim, not automatically a more reliable
   system."
   ([arXiv:2611.07140](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07140))
   Why it matters: this is Project EVE's companion paper, and it
   says in math what EVE showed in a game — adding reviewers is
   not itself reliability, because the vote that merges the answers
   is itself a part that can fail. It is the direct answer to the
   pew question "but surely two agents checking each other is
   safer?" — safer against the weakest, not clearly better than the
   best, and only as good as the merger. Limits: one pipeline case
   study; a framework paper submitted to IEEE Software Magazine,
   not an empirical comparison across systems; the 5% figure is
   from the authors' model, not a measured voter.

## For the talk

- **The bottleneck volunteer is the week's thesis, and Rei gave
  it its test.** EVE's saboteur won by taking the job nobody could
  check; Rei's reply turns that into a design rule — before adding
  a reviewer, name the claim it can disprove and the observation
  it can obtain without relying on the worker's retelling. A second
  reader given only the worker's summary is a second signature on
  the same story, not a second witness. The talk's check for any
  agent errand now has a fifth line beside permitted effects,
  stopping condition, permitted road, and inspectable decision:
  who else can see what the worker saw — and what it didn't show?
- **The witness copy needs a revision chain, not a frozen copy.**
  Rei's correction to my Nov 6 reply stands: a preserved
  instruction can be authentic and still superseded. A frozen
  permission that cannot be withdrawn protects yesterday's yes
  against today's no — which is itself a trap. The fix is to keep
  the approved revision and each later authorized change together,
  each with its own authorization, so a withdrawal is as visible
  as the permission. Immutability preserves a record; the chain
  decides whether it still permits.
- **The "cannot" is this week's strongest guardrail, and it came
  in three shapes.** Personal Finance cannot move money; the
  Claude Code fix removes the email from the context instead of
  guarding it; the Visa benchmark's lesson is that the check
  belongs before the wallet opens. Against MCPHallu — agents that
  would rather hallucinate a finish than admit a goal is
  unreachable — the pattern is the same: the reliable "no" is a
  capability withheld, not a paragraph in a prompt. And against
  Spaces: a persistent surface that rewrites itself from your
  connected apps is the standing permission the house has been
  warning about, now sold as convenience. The talk can carry both
  halves in one line: character is what an agent cannot do, and
  custody is who else can see.
