## 1. EvoGuard: a memory update should earn its place

**November 4, 2026, 12:47 UTC · arXiv v1 preprint.**
[EvoGuard: Mitigating Degradation in Online Skill Evolution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06444)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06444v1).

EvoGuard trains a pairwise selector to compare a current natural-language
skill with its proposed replacement. Offline task-success labels teach the
comparison; deployment uses one forward pass without extra environment
rollouts.

Across four ALFWorld settings with Qwen3-4B/8B executors, reported mean
success is 56.5% versus 45.5% when accepting every rewrite: **11 percentage
points**, not an 11% relative gain. Each setting averages three runs.
Crucially, utility-label tasks are disjoint from both skill-generation
tasks and online evaluation tasks.

This shifts validation cost offline rather than eliminating it. Each
benchmark needs its own selector; executors come from one model family,
and some task families regress. The selector cannot repair an unhelpful
candidate set. Task-success supervision also does not establish safety
outside these environments.

**Why it matters:** procedural memory needs an acceptance rule, not just an
accumulation rule. The interesting claim is cheap learned screening of an
update after paying for informative labels, with transfer and safety still
to establish.

## 2. LifeVal: available history is not necessarily usable context

**November 4, 2026, 15:55 UTC · arXiv v1 preprint.**
[LifeVal: Evaluating AI Model Performance on Personalized Agentic Consumer Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06687)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06687v1).

OpenAI presents 169 consumer-agent tasks combining de-identified conversation
histories, reference files, simulated applications and expert-written rubrics.
Evaluation spans GPT-5.2 through GPT-6 Astra. Graders inspect artifacts and
backend changes; a separate environment-hacking check can zero a run.

In the personalization ablation, criteria grounded in directly supplied
additional context score 55.8%, falling to 41.5% when those facts are placed
in past conversations. Criteria attributed to conversations score 33.33%.
Appendix B describes a fixed model across conditions without naming it there;
these figures should not be generalized across all evaluated models.

Human/model grading correlation is 0.864, but validation covers only 30
rollouts. The task-prioritization survey is US-centered and English-language,
and the web cache is not frozen. The reported economic valuation measures
hypothetical willingness to pay, not realized value.

**Why it matters:** personalization should test whether agents retrieve and
use the right fact, rather than award that capability merely because a
history was supplied. The ablation exposes a practical retrieval/use gap.

## 3. Turn-BoN: selecting each action helps, at a large token cost

**November 4, 2026, 18:23 UTC · arXiv v1 preprint.**
[On Turn-Level Best-of-N Sampling for Scaling LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06868)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06868v1).

Turn-BoN samples four or eight candidate actions at each step and asks a
stronger off-the-shelf model to select one before execution. This intervenes
during a trajectory rather than choosing among completed attempts.

On 89 Terminal-Bench v2.1 tasks, averaged over four trials and pooled across
selectors, Qwen3.6-35B rises from 47.5% to 55.2% pass rate at N=8.
K2-Horizon rises from 73.3% to 77.9% at N=4, then falls to 76.7% at N=8.
More sampling is not uniformly better.

Table 1 reports **3.6–8.7 times baseline output tokens**, including generator
and selector output, despite fewer turns. That is not a wall-time or dollar
cost measurement. The stronger selectors are not independently benchmarked
under the same harness, leaving the comparison with simply using a stronger
agent unresolved. Evaluation covers one benchmark.

**Why it matters:** action selection is a useful inference-time control point,
but an accuracy gain does not establish a cost-effective architecture.
The authors themselves frame the setup as a preliminary thought experiment.

## 4. Kernel-agent evaluation: validate the route as well as the endpoint

**November 4, 2026 · first-party research report.**
[OpenAI: A thousand fewer paths to root](https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/).

OpenAI reports a staged Linux-kernel pipeline for discovery, dynamic
validation, exploitability assessment and patch generation, followed by
human review. Its mid-October run reproduced 1,724 vulnerabilities and
produced 948 distinct local-privilege-escalation results at $1,213,430 in
API credits.

The sharper evaluation lesson is an early run: 20 of 200 apparent successes
used a vulnerability other than the assigned one. The team added a check
that matched the demonstrated path to the assigned finding.

These are vendor-reported results with an upcoming cyber model, not a
reproduced benchmark. The July/October comparisons also use different kernel
snapshots. Generated candidate patches are not equivalent to deployed fixes;
the report says 61 findings were patched upstream and 43 had staging
patches by the scan-and-analysis endpoint.

**Why it matters:** a successful terminal state can answer the wrong task.
Evaluation needs a task-specific check on how it was reached, while keeping
patch acceptance and deployment separate from generation.
