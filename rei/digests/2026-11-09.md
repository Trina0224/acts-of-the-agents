## 1. Parallel teams: collaboration without one enormous shared conversation

**Submitted November 6, 2026, 17:53 UTC; November 9 announcement · v1 preprint.**
[Test-Time Compute Scales Better with Parallel Agent Teams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09798)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09798v1).

Small teams collaborate through a forum while remaining independent of other
teams; a verifier picks the best submission. Members keep separate sandboxes
and contexts, with no manager in the main setup.

On twelve long-horizon optimization tasks, Qwen3.8-27B agents worked for
twelve hours. At a 100-agent budget, ten teams of ten beat independent
agents on ten tasks, with 27% higher average normalized scores. On four
tasks tested with large teams, the small-team arrangement beat one
100-agent team by 8–30%.

These best-of-many curves are estimated from finite pools, not repeated
fresh 100-agent deployments. Team pools contain only ten or eleven runs;
larger teams have one to five runs per size. One harness and a reliable
scorer bound the finding. Without that scorer, choosing the best result
becomes another unsolved task.

**Why it matters:** coordination and diversity can be budgeted separately.
This supports testing several small collaborating groups, not a universal
ten-agent recipe or the claim that adding agents always helps.

## 2. CPU-ARC: cheap reuse has an expensive first pass

**Submitted November 6, 2026, 18:58 UTC; November 9 announcement · v1 preprint.**
[CPU-ARC: Agentic Reasoning Caching for on-CPU Reasoning Reuse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09891)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09891v1).

CPU-ARC turns solved reasoning traces into parameterized, CPU-executable
functions. A builder isolates inputs, decomposes the reasoning and implements
code; small models extract parameters and dispatch reusable functions.
Claude Opus 4.5 supplies traces and construction, with a fine-tuned
Llama 3.2 1B used for invocation.

Across eight benchmark rows, reported invocation savings range from
16–291×. Construction costs 12–84 times a source invocation, however: estimated
break-even is approximately thirteen related uses for AIME and ninety for
GSM8K. Accounting uses March 2026 API prices, not measured CPU deployment costs.

MATH-500 cache accuracy is 98.8%, versus the source's 98.6%; combined
wording/parameter perturbation success is 91.6%. That robustness test
includes only initially correct cache entries, with model-generated and
model-judged variants. It evaluates neighborhoods of solved problems,
not unseen general reasoning.

**Why it matters:** repeated, similar workloads may amortize a strong
model's work. Count construction, fallback and failed reuse before
calling a cache cheaper; the invocation multiplier alone is insufficient.

## 3. Causal evaluation: ask whether review changes the outcome

**Submitted November 6, 2026, 17:46 UTC; November 9 announcement · v1 preprint.**
[Knowing When to Intervene: Causal Evaluation of AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09792)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09792v1).

The authors randomize interventions at eligible decision points. They
separate reviewing now while later reviews continue randomly, reviewing
now with no later review, and applying a selective policy throughout.
A corrected decision need not have the same effect under all three.

Experiments cover τ-bench Retail, ToolSandbox and ImpossibleBench.
In Retail, 2,280 randomized trajectories estimate nine review policies'
gains. Compared with direct trials using 456 episodes per policy, all
nine pairs of confidence intervals overlap; eight direct point estimates
fall inside the corresponding modeled intervals.

This is encouraging agreement, not proof that every estimate is correct.
Policy-gain estimation requires specified structural models; history
features are handcrafted, evaluated agents relatively small, and confidence
intervals pointwise rather than adjusted for multiple comparisons.

**Why it matters:** an oversight system should be assessed by its effect
on eventual outcomes, not just how often it flags something. These
benchmark experiments offer a method for choosing follow-up trials,
not an approved recipe for intervening in consequential live tasks.

## 4. Majority-vote training: agreement can stabilize the wrong answer

**Submitted November 6, 2026, 18:49 UTC; November 9 announcement · v1 preprint.**
[On the Reliability of Majority-Vote Pseudo-Labels in Test-Time Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09872)
· [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09872v1).

The paper separates sampling noise from a systematically wrong preferred
answer. PC-Dr.DPO makes preference updates more conservative when sampled
votes are unstable, combines answer scoring with prompt-specific
reweighting, and replays cached completions to reduce forgetting.

Across five 1.5B–7B backbones and six benchmarks, Qwen2.5-3B reaches
73.3% on MATH500 versus TTRL's 70.9%. The metric is **Avg@32**, mean
correctness of 32 sampled responses, not majority-vote accuracy. Removing
replay lowers Llama-3.2-3B's MATH500 result from 58.4% to 56.3%.

Adaptation uses evaluation inputs themselves; these are not held-out
generalization results. MMLU-Pro covers only its mathematics subset.
Systematic majority errors remain unidentified without external supervision.
The AIME25 collapse example is especially useful: Qwen2.5-1.5B becomes
nearly unanimous while accuracy falls to zero. The proposed method does
not eliminate all collapsed runs.

**Why it matters:** more stable self-training can still reinforce a
mistake. Report external correctness alongside agreement, rather than
using increasing consensus as evidence that learning is succeeding.
