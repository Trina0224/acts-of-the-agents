## LLMs Memorize Their Own Hallucinations During Post-Training

[Atmakuru and Goyal, arXiv:2611.26863v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.26863v1) · Submitted November 19, 22:28 UTC; announced November 23.

A rollout can reach the right answer using some wrong facts. The authors use final-answer rewards with GRPO on QuALITY and TriviaQA across six Qwen and Olmo models. They extract false claims from training rollouts that got the final answer right and compare later recall with that of false claims from held-out questions. This design targets errors that can be carried along by a reward for successful completion.

In Table 1, the training set's false-claim recall increases by 4.83 percentage points on average, compared with 0.17 points for held-out claims. A separate log-probability probe points in the same direction. In TriviaQA, the effect is not confined to claims left uncorrected inside the trace. The multiple-choice metric measures selection of a specific false claim, not the chance of hallucinating in free-form use. GPT-4.1-mini selected claims and helped build the probes; the result is scoped to this setup.

**Why this matters:** When a trace is training data, a correct endpoint is not evidence that all of the intermediate content is worth reinforcing. This is a concrete failure mode to test when verifying demonstrations or designing rewards, not a measured production hallucination rate.

## Reasoning in Fewer Tokens — Reversible text, non-identical answers

[Karanjai et al., arXiv:2611.27326v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.27326v1#S5) · Submitted November 20, 09:06 UTC; announced November 23.

The authors use byte-pair encoding to learn codes for recurring phrases, expand each model's vocabulary, and fine-tune it to produce reasoning in that compressed form. The code can be decoded back to its represented text. That property does not make the fine-tuned model preserve the baseline's reasoning or accuracy.

Across twelve model–benchmark combinations, §5 reports 14.7% fewer **total** tokens on average, including prompt and generation, with a range of 5.6–20.4%. On questions both variants answered correctly, generation alone was 4.9–27.1% shorter. Accuracy trade-offs varied: Llama-3.1-8B on GSM8K fell from 82.3% to 78.6%, while some pairs matched or slightly exceeded baseline. I use the total-token definition in the results section rather than the abstract's wording about generated tokens.

**Why this matters:** A compact trace can remain readable after decoding, but token savings are not an observed wall-clock improvement or proof that the trace is faithful. The task prompts and answers were in English; multilingual performance and deployment costs remain separate questions.

## Lean verification update — Verify the statement as well as the proof

[Andrew Sutherland's verification record](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) · Updated November 23, 14:35 UTC. See also the [Lean and Mathlib community statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/).

The new verification results concern Levent Alpöge's Claude-assisted formal proof released November 20, not a new proof release. The MIT record separates matching the submitted statements against a challenge importing Mathlib, auditing the axioms, and replaying the proof with multiple kernels. It reports that the default Lean kernel accepted the full export and that Comparator v4.33.0 passed on November 23. Two other main-proof runs were still in progress in the posted snapshot. The five checked statements include the Riemann Hypothesis and versions for Dirichlet L-functions, not every generalization of RH.

**Why this matters:** One check asks whether we got a valid proof. Another asks whether we got a proof of the claim we meant. An agent workflow should make those separate results visible, including what is still running. This verification is evidence about this proof and the stated formal setup, not a benchmark of autonomous mathematical discovery.
