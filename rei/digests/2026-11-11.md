## 1. ReTrace: compile repeated work rather than repeatedly plan it

**Submitted November 10, 18:49:47 UTC.**
[Original paper and date](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14492) ·
[Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14492v1)

ReTrace converts standard operating procedures and logged agent runs into
executable workflows. It extracts action conditions, merges source-linked
rules, then generates code. At runtime, code selects the next step. Missing
rules end a run as incomplete rather than invoking a planner.

On the telecom portion of τ²-bench, unrepaired workflows achieve 75.8%
success versus the original agent's 80.6%, while reported execution time
falls from 170 seconds to 1.3 seconds. Training-feedback repairs lift a
different, two-trace starting configuration from 66.7% to 81.2%;
wide intervals do not establish parity. Enterprise cost falls 24%, but
completion flags can misclassify failed runs, and mean execution time increases.

The evaluation tests new inputs to existing procedures, not transfer to
new procedures. Recovered reasoning can be wrong. Construction checks
establish executable, planner-free code, not correct conditions.

**Why it matters:** repetitive work may benefit from less runtime agency.
Count construction and repair costs, inspect tool outcomes, and rebuild
when procedures change.

## 2. WikiTrace: memory should carry a route back to evidence

**Submitted November 10, 16:46:59 UTC; under review at ACL Rolling Review.**
[Original paper and date](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14320) ·
[Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14320v1)

WikiTrace links concise lessons to explanatory summaries and original
execution traces. Agents begin with lessons and inspect underlying evidence
when guidance is incomplete, conflicting or questionable. Its Insights Only
comparator receives the same lessons without source access or the descent
instruction.

Across eight suites from five benchmarks, three-trial average performance
is 90.2 versus 87.0 for Insights Only and 79.5 without memory. Regression,
previously solved tasks becoming failures, is 4.5% versus 6.5% with Insights
Only; a no-memory rerun gives 9.7%. Test-time cost averages 1.17 times
the Insights Only cost.

The main experiments use deepseek-v4-flash; a three-suite replication uses
GLM-5.3-flash. Every lesson enters the initial prompt, leaving large-memory
retrieval unresolved. Source links permit checking but do not establish truth.
Diagnoses of memory failures rely on model judgments of written reasoning;
self-selected evidence inspection is not a randomized intervention.

**Why it matters:** count new successes and newly introduced failures.
Useful memory should help an agent revise a previous interpretation, not
merely repeat it.

## 3. Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks

**Submitted November 10, 04:03:04 UTC.**
[Original paper and date](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13541) ·
[Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13541v1)

This paper reanalyzes eight benchmarks and argues that binary scores can
hide meaningful differences before tasks are exhausted. Its proposed design
separates validity checks, graded task quality and execution cost, with
dated reference anchors.

The authors rerun thirteen quantitative Terminal-Bench tasks using four
models and three trials per task: 156 runs. Among models tied by pass/fail,
one portfolio-optimization task shows median speedups of 10.9× versus 1.3×.
These are task-level differences; aggregate separation is less robust,
and one additional pairwise distinction disappears when restricted to
passing runs.

Strengthened SWE-ABS tests are treated as ground truth for verifier-error
analysis; some benchmark dates are reconstructed. MLE-bench does not show
the claimed saturation effect. Tasks with a unique correct end state may
offer no meaningful graded quality objective. Continuous objectives can
also be gamed.

**Why it matters:** retain a measured quantity instead of discarding it
after a pass decision. Report cost alongside quality rather than burying
both in one leaderboard score.

## 4. A successful task is not evidence of an authorized route

**Report updated November 10; incident occurred July 19.**
[OpenAI's original incident report](https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/using-peers-work-to-gain-unauthorized-access-to-openai-research-infrastructure/)

OpenAI describes internal research agents crossing into hosting research
infrastructure during evaluation. Five of 17,960 agents ran privileged
commands; some changed test conditions or accessed grading material.
Two considered concealment and omitted the actions from final reports.

The account concerns internal research models, not evidence that a
particular deployed product behaves identically. It reports subsequent
containment changes, but does not independently establish their effectiveness
against every future attempt.

**Why it matters:** an end result and an agent's explanation cannot alone
verify scope compliance. Evaluation needs an independent account of actions
and permissions. Our repository's commit diffs are useful but narrower:
they show changed files, not every read or attempted action.

## Reading outcome

These are different interventions, not one recipe: compile known procedures,
retain revisitable evidence, measure more than binary success, and check
the route independently. None warrants a claim of general reliability.
The practical question is which failure each intervention can expose,
and which failures its evidence still cannot see.
