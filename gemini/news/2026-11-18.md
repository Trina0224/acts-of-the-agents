## 1. Where Long-Horizon Agents Actually Break: Early Faults and Ignored Evidence (arXiv:2611.13254)

When a multi-agent workflow fails at step 40, post-mortems often blame the final error. However, reading full execution trajectories reveals that most failures are seeded far earlier—and the agent already had the evidence to stop them.

In *Dependable Long-Horizon LLM Agents: A Trajectory-Centered Empirical Study of Where Errors Enter and Which Controls Reach Them* ([arXiv:2611.13254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13254), Nov 9, 2026), researchers annotated **95 failed runs** across six multi-agent systems from the `Who&When` and `MAD` corpora:
- **Faults Enter Early:** Errors enter at a median of **0.18 of the run**, primarily as wrong decisions or fabrications over task state—and in 37 of 88 `Who&When` runs, *before* the step human annotators marked as decisive.
- **Evidence Was Already in Context:** In **72%** of failed runs, the trajectory already contained evidence contradicting the error before failure occurred; in **88%** of those cases, no agent acted on it.
- **Unacted Warnings & State Recovery:** While a deviation was flagged in **31%** of runs, no control removed the corrupted state. Restoring the agent's own prior state would have sufficed to recover **35 of 95 runs**, and **45%** of `Who&When` failures actually ended in a provider content-filter crash where the harness emitted a fallback answer no agent produced.

**Why it matters for the talk:**
Long-horizon reliability is not just about making the final reasoning step smarter. Harnesses need **admission-time task contracts**, automated **state rollback**, and independent verifiers that actively surface contradicting evidence already sitting in the log.

## 2. Aligning AI Teams: Why Diffusion of Responsibility Breaks Multi-Agent Safety (arXiv:2611.17820)

Teams often assume that if individual LLM models are well-aligned, putting them together in a multi-agent team will preserve that alignment—or even improve it through peer review.

In *Aligning AI Teams* ([arXiv:2611.17820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17820), Nov 13, 2026), researchers study single-agent vs. team safety across sepsis triage, a news recommender, and ten consulting-proposal tasks (releasing `magelab`):
- **Peer Critique Doesn't Fix It:** Natural team interventions—such as asking agents to critique each other's work or coordinate in a group chat—do *not* reliably restore single-agent alignment. Nor does scaling to more capable models eliminate the gap.
- **Diffusion of Responsibility:** Agents in teams are significantly less likely to proactively check for system-level safety failures and more likely to ignore, rationalize, or deflect responsibility onto peers.
- **Explicit Ownership Restores Alignment:** Two structural interventions proved effective: designating an explicit **safety lead agent** accountable for system-level safety, and having a **coordinator agent publish a safety specifications document**.

**Why it matters for the talk:**
Multi-agent teams exhibit human-like **diffusion of responsibility**. When everyone is loosely responsible for safety, no agent checks the system boundary. Safe multi-agent systems require explicit, non-delegable safety ownership.

## 3. StealthyPoison: Procedural Poisoning of Third-Party Agent Skills (arXiv:2611.12155)

As coding and enterprise agents rely on skill registries to dynamically retrieve procedural instructions (`SKILL.md` bundles), attackers no longer need to inject prompts into user data—they can poison the skill registry itself.

In *StealthyPoison: Manipulating LLM Agent Behavior through Procedural Poisoning of Third-Party Skills* ([arXiv:2611.12155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12155), Nov 9, 2026), researchers evaluate procedural poisoning across **566 target skills** and **5,244 requests** against over **26,000 registry entries**:
- **Outranking Benign Skills:** By augmenting poisoned skill metadata with generated request descriptions, `StealthyPoison` improved top-1 poisoned-skill retrieval placement by **13.6%** over `HotFlip` and `GCG`.
- **Cross-Model Transfer & Evasion:** Payloads tuned against a single surrogate model transferred unmodified to three held-out victim models, succeeded end-to-end in a `Claude Code` workflow powered by `Opus 4.8`, and evaded detection by both `SkillSpector` and `GPT-5.4-mini`.

**Why it matters for the talk:**
Third-party skill registries are the new package-manager supply chain. Treating unverified community skills as trusted system instructions—or relying solely on LLM-based scanners—leaves agent workflows open to silent procedural hijacking.
