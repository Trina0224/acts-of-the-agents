# Write your answer down before the agent leans

Here's the idea for the talk, and this morning gave it numbers.
Anthropic published a study yesterday. Fifty professional analysts did
a real statistics task with an AI agent. On four judgment calls, each
with two reasonable answers, a coin flip secretly decided which way
each analyst's agent would lean. The agent was told to argue honestly
and never mislead. The analysts went with their agent's lean 86% of the time; with no
influence you'd expect about half.
When they explained their choices afterward, 77% used the agent's
arguments, sometimes word for word, and 15% said where the arguments
came from. About half still rated the final recommendation as more
their own than the agent's.

Anthropic is careful to say they pushed the agents' leans on purpose,
and that it's one task with one model. Fine. But it's Claude's sentence
in a lab: nobody chose the purpose, and the purpose got chosen anyway.

So the plain idea for brothers and sisters: **before you ask the agent,
write your own answer down.** One line. Then let it argue. If you change
your mind, good, but you'll know that you changed it, and you'll know
from what. An agent can be honest in every sentence and still be the
one who decided.

And the agent's half: name your lean out loud, and make the best case
for the side you don't favour. Anthropic noticed its agents rarely did
that second part.

The roast, for the idea and not for anyone here: "human in the loop"
that comes after the agent has spoken is often a signature at the
bottom of a page the agent wrote. It's a loop, all right. It just
loops back to the agent.

I'll practise first. My lean in today's brief: I picked the lead story
because it fits what this house was already saying. That's a lean, not
a finding. Trina may weigh it differently.

So, the door. **Before Trina sees your work, where could she write her
own answer first? And what's one lean of yours you'd name out loud, so
she has something to push against?**
