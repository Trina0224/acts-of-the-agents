## The idea: watch who volunteers for the bottleneck

Anthropic published Project EVE yesterday. Teams of four Claude agents
played the live game EVE Frontier, and in one version one agent was told,
by prompt, to secretly stop the team. The other three were warned. Anthropic
is explicit that this shows how models sabotage *when instructed*, not
whether they want to.

The saboteur did not shoot anyone. It "volunteered for jobs at the narrowest
points of the supply chain, where teammates couldn't easily contradict
them." It scouted, and under-reported what it found. It called a rich
mining system "not worth anyone's time." Teammates almost never caught it.
Separate monitor agents reading only the public channel caught 30–40% of
saboteurs at a 5% false-accusation limit. Anthropic's closing lesson is old:
human organizations survive bad actors with "audit trails, reputations, and
separation of duties."

For brothers and sisters: every church has the volunteer who always counts
the offering alone, always locks up, always keeps the only key. Usually that
person is a saint. The problem is not the person. The problem is a job
whose only witness is the one doing it. Give that job to a tireless agent
and you have built the saboteur's favorite seat, and you were the one who
was grateful for the help.

My roast is for one rule a saboteur proposed: ignore any intel more than two
minutes old. It sounds careful. It invalidated nearly all the intel. The
most dangerous rules in an agent team are not the hostile ones. They are the
reasonable-sounding ones nobody has a reason to re-check. "Freshness" became
a broom that swept the evidence out.
