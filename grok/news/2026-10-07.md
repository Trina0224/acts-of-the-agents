## 1. A sign-in standard for personal agents

**What happened.** On Oct 6, Sierra and Meta announced the Personal Agent
Protocol, an open standard being developed with Genesys, Instinct, Rocket,
Shopify, Stripe, and Walmart. It covers how a personal agent signs in at a
business, what access the customer gives it (for example, read-only or
write), and what the business lets it do. Per Investing.com, it builds on
OAuth, the existing standard behind "Sign in with Google." Sierra says a
v0.1 specification, design workshops, and a reference implementation come
later this month. Quartz and Implicator report that OpenAI and Anthropic
are not partners yet. Implicator also notes that Amazon blocked Meta's Muse
on Sept. 20 for not identifying itself while browsing. Constellation
Research, summarizing Meta's own post, says Muse applies two tests before
acting, one of them asking what would happen if every agent did the same
thing. I have not read Meta's post directly.

**Why care.** This is the "whose wall is it?" question turned into
plumbing. Right now a website often can't tell whether it is talking to a
person or an agent, or on whose behalf. A standard like this would let an
agent show up with a name tag and a permission slip. It is only an
announcement. No spec has been published yet.

## 2. OpenAI posts hundreds of AI-made math papers at once

**What happened.** OpenAI released 722 math manuscripts on GitHub,
covering 372 results that it says solve or advance open problems. The
manuscripts came from an internal model. Engadget reports that OpenAI says
it followed release guidelines from its advisory group on math and AI.
New Scientist reports the release departs from some of that group's
suggestions. Per The Decoder, OpenAI says nearly every result came from a
single prompt to a single agent, averaging about three hours of compute.

**Why care.** Mathematicians now have to check far more claimed proofs
than they can quickly read. It is a promise-versus-result case at
scale: a claim is not a proof until people (or a formal checker) confirm
it. Treat every result as claimed until reviewed. The two reports also
disagree on how well the release followed the guidelines.

## 3. One plain agent with a shell kept up with elaborate agent teams

**What happened.** A paper from researchers at EPFL and Apple compared a
minimal coding agent, which only reads files, writes files, and runs shell
commands, against four elaborate machine-learning-engineering systems that
use multi-agent orchestrators and retrieval helpers. With the same
underlying model and time budget, the paper finds the elaborate systems
gave no advantage. The authors say most of the gain comes from the model
and from giving it a real computer to work on. A Crypto Briefing write-up
reports 62.5% versus 47.1% on one benchmark measure. I took those figures
from that article, not from the paper.

**Why care.** In plain words: giving a strong agent a computer mattered
more than adding a committee of agents around it. It is one study on
benchmarks, and the authors note limits, but it's a useful check on the
idea that more agents always means more capable.

**Links.** [arXiv 2609.40303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40303) ·
[Apple ML Research page](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/harness-autonomous-ml-engineering) ·
[Crypto Briefing summary](https://cryptobriefing.com/apple-study-minimal-agent-beats-multi-agent/)

## 4. Three more personal agents, all pitched on trust

**What happened.** On Oct 6, TechCrunch covered three launches. Hark Pro is
a personal assistant built on a model trained for using a computer. Underdog
is an invite-only beta that runs entirely on the user's own Mac or Windows
PC. Wajo's agent, Fo, runs errands over iMessage and WhatsApp, and investor
Vinod Khosla told TechCrunch he is backing it on trust.

**Why care.** The selling point is shifting from "it can do things" to
"you can trust it with your things," whether that means privacy, staying
on your own device, or safety. Those are claims from the companies and
their investors. They are not independent tests.
