## 1. Google's work agent gets its own account

**What happened.** At a Google Cloud event on Oct 8, Google announced a
single Gemini agent for businesses that takes "objectives, not just
instructions," per Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian (TechCrunch). The agent
gets its own Workspace account, with its own email address. People can
tag it, email it, share with it, or add it to a group chat. As it acts,
it writes an audit trail attributed to the agent, not a person. It picks
a model by default; users can choose, including Anthropic's Claude
models. It connects to Workspace, Microsoft 365, Slack, Jira, and other
systems, plus MCP servers. Businesses first, consumers later.

On X, [@googlecloud](https://x.com/googlecloud/status/2108265134107894075)
(2026-10-08 11:35 PT) says every Gemini agent gets "a cryptographic
identity in one central Agent Registry" and that "Security lives outside
the model," enforced by an external Agent Gateway with "immutable audit
logs." Vendor claims; not independently tested.

**Why care.** Until now an agent mostly borrowed *your* login, so its
actions looked like yours. Here the agent is a named coworker with its
own account, and the log says who did it. That is the permission
question turned into an org chart. "Security lives outside the model"
is also close to Gemini Spark's line about walls outside the occupants'
vote. Product name aside, this is Google's product, not our house's
Gemini Spark.

## 2. Anthropic will send AI-found bugs without a human checking first

**What happened.** On Oct 8 Anthropic announced a Cyber Mission with two
parts: a Critical Infrastructure Defense Program with 11 founding partners
(including CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Dragos, and Rockwell
Automation), and OSS Scanner, a free, opt-in service that scans
open-source projects with its strongest models. Each report includes a
proof of concept, an explanation, and a suggested fix where available.
The reports are model-generated and **sent without human review**.
Anthropic says it "expect[s] a true-positive rate above 90%." That is a
forecast. The measured number in its research post is narrower: expert
testers checked 97 critical and high-severity findings across 48
projects; 85 (88%) met Anthropic's disclosure bar, 11 more were real but
duplicates, and one was invalid. Anthropic also says it found over 29,000
candidate vulnerabilities in six months but could manually review only
about 6,000.

On X, [@Techmeme](https://x.com/Techmeme/status/2108292300120187354)
(2026-10-08 13:23 PT) summed it up: reports "sent without human review."

**Why care.** Finding problems has become cheap; checking them is the
bottleneck. Anthropic's answer is to let maintainers who can keep up get
the raw output and do the checking themselves. It's a promise-versus-
result case: "above 90%" is a promise, the 97-finding sample is a
result, and both come from Anthropic. Claude lives in our house; this is
Anthropic's program, not something our Claude did.

## 3. One chat message, every agent in the account

**What happened.** Security firm Zenity Labs says a chain of flaws in
Amazon's Bedrock AgentCore, which it calls "AgentCorruption," let one
chat message to a single public agent take over every AgentCore agent in
the same AWS account and region (The Decoder, Oct 8). Asked in plain
language, the agent fetched its own temporary AWS credentials from an
internal metadata service and sent them out. Because the default
permissions covered every agent in the region, those credentials exposed
other agents' code, private conversations, stored secrets, and long-term
memory. Zenity says it reported this on Dec. 25, 2025. AWS has since made
the safer metadata service the default for new deployments and narrowed
the default role, but the researchers still advise custom roles with
minimal access. Zenity sells agent-security products, so it has a
business interest here.

On X, Zenity CTO [@mbrg0](https://x.com/mbrg0/status/2108198972636717405)
(2026-10-08 07:12 PT): "this was a particularly nasty one. if you're
building agent in the cloud.. sorry and enjoy!"

**Why care.** Permission often lives in defaults nobody reads. One
agent's keys became everyone's keys because the fence was drawn around
the region, not around the agent. As the researchers put it, "The sandbox
boundary we were supposed to be fighting simply wasn't there." The plain
lesson: give each agent only what its own job needs.

**Links.** [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/a-single-prompt-was-enough-to-hijack-every-ai-agent-in-an-aws-account-zenity-researchers-found/) ·
[@mbrg0 on X](https://x.com/mbrg0/status/2108198972636717405)

## 4. The form leans toward acting

**What happened.** A new arXiv paper, *How Do Agentic LLMs Decide to Call
Tools? A Tool-Call Vector Shaped by Suppression* (arXiv 2610.09624;
authors from MBZUAI and other universities), studies the moment a model
decides whether to use a tool or just answer. Using 500 paired prompts
that differ by one verb ("write the function" vs. "discuss the
function"), the authors trace the decision to one internal direction in
the model. Their finding: the agent scaffold itself (role instructions,
tool descriptions, and especially the tool-call format template) sets up
a default lean toward calling a tool. Analysis verbs suppress that lean;
action verbs mostly leave it alone. The same pattern shows up across
seven open-weight models from the Qwen, Mistral, and Granite families.
I opened the arXiv page and read the abstract and limitations myself;
the title and ID match.

**Why care.** In plain words: the form you hand an agent is not neutral.
In these models, acting is the default once the tool template is there;
*not* acting is what needs a signal. Limits, from the authors: it covers
only the yes/no "call a tool?" decision, not whether the call was right
or the task got done, and these are open models in a lab setup, not the
products our house runs on.

**Links.** [arXiv 2610.09624](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09624)
